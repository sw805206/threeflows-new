Is a $2 or a $20 Product Better for Your Business?

A quick unit economics walk-down, the "free shipping" trap, and how to tell whether a product is worth your time to sell.

November 15, 2025

If you're buying a $2 or a $20 product from a vendor to resell, you want to know how much you should mark it up to make a profit — and, ultimately, whether it's worth your time to do at all.

The honest answer starts with "it depends" — because there are a lot of ifs and buts. Let's start simple: break down the detailed cost stack for one unit — calculate the unit economics, the per-unit P&L, and understand the cost of goods sold (COGS) structure.

The Six Variable Cost Categories — and How to Reduce Them

1. Product cost. A $2 product might be something like a coaster or a small coin pouch. A $20 product might be a coffee maker or a folding chair. You can find both on Alibaba, but the secret is 1688.com if you can navigate the language barrier — it's Alibaba's Chinese domestic version, with roughly 10× more products than Alibaba, meaningfully cheaper prices, and fresh designs that typically appear about three months before they surface elsewhere. However, quality and packaging are hit or miss, which is why sampling matters.

2. Shipping and tariff. If you have volume and can absorb the wait, ocean freight is the answer — roughly $3 per kg. Air freight is a lot faster but a lot more expensive. Tariff depends on the product category and current US policy. For illustration, we'll use 30% of product cost. Therefore, on a $2 product at 0.5 kg, that's about $1.50 shipping + $0.60 tariff. On a $20 product at 5 kg, it's about $15 + $6.

3. Storage. Once your goods arrive in the US, they need somewhere to sit. A small light product might fit in your garage — effectively free until you outgrow it. A larger product will need a paid storage unit or a 3PL warehouse — they charge by product volume and how long it sits there — typically $5-15 per unit across a 4-month holding period for that $20 product.

4. Selling cost. Whether you sell online or offline, this line is significant — usually 20-50% of your selling price. It includes the platform's referral or commission (Amazon: ~15%; Shopify: lower base but you pay for traffic yourself), plus advertising to actually get the product in front of buyers (typically another 15-25%). Retail and wholesale channels replace platform fees with trade margins in the same 30-50% range. Skipping ads is possible; skipping fees is not.

5. Fulfillment. Pick, pack, label, and last-mile delivery. Pick and pack is "free" if you're doing it yourself, typically $1-2 per order at a 3PL. Last-mile parcel delivery is where the real money goes: $7-30 per shipment for B2C, depending on weight and zone. Parcel rate aggregators can save 35-75% off retail carrier rates, though the actual discount depends on volume, package profile, and negotiation. (This is one of the places where getting outside help pays off — contact us if you want a look at your rate structure.) Selling through retail stores or a flea market eliminates last-mile cost entirely — though you'll pick up other costs in exchange (retail trade margin, booth or shelf fees, staffing).

6. Loss, damage, and returns. Return rates typically run 5-20% depending on category. Once returned, it's unlikely you can resell at full price — so treat the return cost as full unit loss plus any return shipping. For illustration, we'll use 5% of selling price.

The "Free Shipping" Trap

Everyone offers free shipping when selling online. But we all know there's no free lunch. In fact, "free shipping" is a sales pitch. The cost is always baked into the selling price. The consumer pays for the convenience one way or another; the seller's job is to find shipping cheap enough that neither party bears too much of the burden.

On the flip side, last-mile is the only cost the seller has real control over as a strategic lever. Every other line is largely set by the market — factory prices, tariffs, platform fees. Shipping is the one you can either charge (recovering the cost, at the price of some conversion) or absorb (killing the margin, but lifting the conversion). "Free shipping" isn't a checkbox; it's a strategic choice that changes the whole math.

Put the six categories together and you can back into a breakeven markup — how many times your ex-factory cost you need to charge just to break even, before margin. Two rough ratios worth carrying:

  • A $2 product breaks even at roughly 4× ex-factory ($8 selling) if the buyer pays shipping, and around 10× ex-factory ($20 selling) if you offer free shipping.
  • A $20 product breaks even at roughly 4.5× ex-factory ($90 selling) with paid shipping, and around 6× ($120 selling) with free shipping.

Caveats for Unit Economics

The breakeven walk-down above is useful, but it's a partial picture. Three things it doesn't capture:

  • Only per-unit variable costs (COGS) are in here. OPEX (rent, staff, subscriptions, insurance) and CAPEX (equipment, tooling, product development, launch spend) sit outside the per-unit math and still have to be paid — and covered by margin from the units you sell.
  • Volume changes the math, in both directions. Larger orders raise your upfront cost, but they lower nearly every unit cost line — freight rates, 3PL rates, ads efficiency, platform fee tiers, returns processing. The lever that reconciles the two is velocity: how fast you can sell what you buy.
  • Unit economics don't show you cash flow. Most of the costs above are paid weeks or months before the first sale. Whether a good unit-economics product is also a good cash-flow product is a different question — and one we'll cover in the next post.

Don't Get Discouraged

Every cost above is knowable, calculable, and optimizable. Suppliers can be negotiated. Shipping rates can be brought down. Storage can be right-sized. Ad efficiency can be tuned. The mechanical side of unit economics rewards patience and discipline — none of it is mysterious.

What makes the difference between a product that sells and one that doesn't is the velocity enablers: a genuinely unique product idea, an audience you know how to reach, an existing sales channel or community you can activate. If any of those are true for you, the cost stack becomes a solvable problem. If none of them are true yet, that's the work — not the math.

When you're just getting started, you have no clue what will work. Invest as little as possible, DIY as much as possible, and get first-hand experience — that's how you validate whether the cost structure above actually fits your specific product. Once you have a real signal, the optimization work above becomes worth doing.
