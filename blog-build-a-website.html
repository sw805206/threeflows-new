Website is your business card. How do you make one?

The first thing a customer checks is your website. Building one yourself teaches you more about your business — and in 2026, it no longer takes a developer.

June 6, 2026

Sometimes you cannot start without one

A website has quietly become a credential. Payment processors ask for a live URL during account review. Marketplaces and wholesale accounts ask for one in the application. Suppliers, landlords and loan officers look you up before they commit.

None of them are shopping. They are verifying that a business exists at all. So a website is not the reward you give yourself once the business works. It is often the gate you pass through before it can start.

What building it teaches you

As a business owner, building a website is not only for the public. Dare I say it is for you.

Even the simplest single-page site has to tell a stranger who you are, what you do, what you offer, what value you bring, why you and not someone else, why they should trust you, and how to reach you. It sounds simple, and most owners believe they have the answers. It feels different once all of it is on one page, ready for anyone to read — and keeping that reader to the end is a separate problem again.

Which is exactly why the exercise is worth doing yourself. It forces you to examine the business: to put the flagship products and services where they belong, to cut the long tail that no longer earns its keep, to think twice about who you are trying to attract, and to look hard at how quickly a visitor turns into a sale.

The real 2026 story is discovery, not adoption

Search stopped sending traffic. In the first four months of 2026, just over 68% of Google searches ended without a click (SparkToro and Datos). Pew Research found that when an AI summary sits at the top of the results, people click through on 8% of visits, against 15% without one.

Meanwhile AI became a discovery channel almost overnight. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found AI tools jumped from 6% to 45% of local business discovery in a single year, and 42% of consumers now trust an AI recommendation about as much as a written review.

Your site used to be a destination you drove traffic to. Now it is also the source answer engines read when someone asks about your category — or about you by name. If your facts are not there in a form a machine can cite, you are not in the answer.

Which is why an email address at gmail.com no longer reads as thrifty. It reads as absent.

Now the build options

Three categories, in order of commitment.

From no website to a domain of your own

Google Sites costs nothing and can be live in an afternoon — and if you already run Google Workspace, it is included in what you pay.

It suits two kinds of business.

The first is an established company that does not depend on the internet for promotion, but wants somewhere to organize and list its services, and to turn what visitors type into something manageable at the back end — booking, order taking, a service list. A hair salon or a pet care business, for instance.

The second is a startup still in conceptualization or early launch, where the owner is working out the company identity and the shape of the offer. Here the site is a prototype with a small audience of friends and family: enough to gather feedback and to get the process established.

Website builders: a template that goes a long way

GoDaddy, Wix, Squarespace and Shopify are the popular four. Entry tiers run roughly $10–40 a month.

GoDaddy is the easiest and cheapest of the four, and you can buy the domain and build the site in the same place. The experience is a little like Google Workspace — straightforward, not especially versatile. It works best when your message is simple and direct.

Wix is our recommendation. It is versatile, relatively easy to use, and generous with what it includes: a large image library, forms, a logo generator, business card templates. It is still a template system, so you work inside their design language. Get creative and you will hit rocks.

Squarespace built its name on credible-looking pages. Beyond that, we have yet to find its core competency.

Shopify is the most expensive, for a reason. It is the right answer for e-commerce, particularly with a long product list, and overkill if you are not selling online. If you own a brand, though, it matters: a brand that lives only on a marketplace like amazon.com is neither credible nor safe, because the platform can shut you down.

Switching platforms is only half easy. The domain moves in an afternoon, because it is yours. The site does not — hosted platforms rarely let you export content or styling, so you rebuild from scratch. You own the address. You rent the building.

Fully customized, assisted by AI or a human

This is where WordPress has always lived. It runs more of the web than the four builders combined, but it is not a product you sign up for — it is software you assemble: hosting, theme, plugins, backups, patches. Before 2026 it was the natural upgrade when you wanted customization. There is now an easier way in.

Running your own site is easier and cheaper than most owners assume, and it is the only route where the design answers entirely to you.

The setup: buy a domain, which you pay for on every route anyway, and point it at a free Cloudflare account to handle DNS — the traffic control tower for your domain. From there it is HTML pages, built with AI or a programmer.

What you get is ownership. The files are yours, so moving hosts is a copy-and-paste. Nothing is cookie-cutter, because nothing started as a template. And logic stops being a special case — a calculator is just another file.

This site is the example. threeflows.com is hand-built static HTML on free hosting. It is not apple.com and does not pretend to be. But everything holding it together — a consistent set of colors and type sizes, a shared header and footer, tools that calculate in your browser — is reachable by someone with no coding background working with an AI assistant. The constraint was never talent. The route simply did not exist until 2026.

If you want the polish of a big-name company's site, hire designers and programmers — expect $5,000 and up to get it set up with outsourced professionals. The real cost, though, is the maintenance. A site like apple.com is run by a department. It is an ongoing operating expense, not a one-time project.

The middle ground is the right combination: your content; a capable human who needs no coding skill but knows your business; code written with AI; and professional applications — Canva, Stripe, SendGrid — for the capabilities you are not going to build yourself. The people bring the soul and the connection. The tools do the legwork.

Either way, they are good at executing page launches and no help at all with your vision. The ceiling is your clarity about your own business. The floor is the time between now, when you decide to start, and launch, when the public needs to see an MVP.

Conclusion

The scores below are our own assessment, not an industry standard — treat them as a reference point rather than a verdict. Effort and customization run 1 to 5, where 1 is least and 5 is most. A domain costs roughly $15 a year on every route, so it is left out.

Category Option Effort Custom Cost
Better than nothing Google Workspace 1 1 Free
Website builder GoDaddy 1 2 $10–25/mo
Wix 2 3 $17–40/mo
Squarespace 2 3 $16–49/mo
Shopify 2 3 $39/mo+
Coding involved WordPress 4 4 $100–500/yr hosting
AI assisted 3 4 $500+ if you hire someone to help
Programmer assisted 5 5 $5,000+

Our recommendations, for the money:

  • Google Workspace — proving a concept, or an established business that does not need anything fancy and does not depend on SEO to be found.
  • Wix — the website builder to pick when you want to run it yourself and do not need deep customization.
  • Shopify — e-commerce brand owners.
  • Assisted build — content-rich businesses that keep evolving, and are not selling online. The combination that works: you supply the content, someone coordinates between you and the AI, and the AI does the building. The bill is small. Your time is the real cost, and learning to work with AI is not foolproof.
