It's not only about Iran's gas price

Someone posted a photo from a gas pump in Iran, and the comments filled with people trying to work out the price in dollars per gallon. Almost nobody could.

August 10, 2026

A friend picked Iran as her summer trip destination. The pictures she posted were stunning: the culture, the food, the people. One of them caught everyone's eye and drew a pile of comments, and it was not a monument or a meal. It was a random shot of a local gas pump on August 3rd.

She had filled 30 liters for 45,000 tomans.

I am fairly sure that everyone who saw her post went online to search what that equates to in dollars per gallon.

Behind every Google or AI search sit three steps, and each one is a place to go wrong if you do it by hand:

  1. Volume. Liters to US gallons.
  2. Currency. Tomans to rials, then rials to US dollars.
  3. Exchange rate. The USD-to-rial rate on 2026-08-01.

This post is not about politics. The inability to know the answer right away is the topic today. And here is the reason.

The measuring system, one of the things that set the US apart

The US is one of three countries that have not adopted the metric system as their official standard. The other two are Liberia and Myanmar. Everywhere else, the base units are the same ones.

A few of these have company. Letter-size paper is also standard in Canada, Mexico, and parts of Latin America. Fahrenheit survives in a handful of Caribbean territories. The UK keeps pints and stones in daily speech while trading in metric. But on the whole, the column on the right is the world your suppliers, carriers, and customers live in.

The trap inside trap

We tend to call the US system the imperial system and everyone else's the metric system. That shorthand hides two real hazards.

US customary units are not British imperial units. A US gallon is 3.785 liters; an imperial gallon is 4.546. A US pint is 16 fluid ounces; an imperial pint is 20. Two documents can both say "gallon" and mean quantities that differ by a fifth, and both parties can be reading their own document correctly.

In some industries, the US is already metric. The ones that work in step with the rest of the world include pharmaceuticals, medicine, and nutrition; science and engineering research; the military; automotive manufacturing; and electronics and semiconductors.

So a 5-foot-10 American runs a 5K, drinks a 32-ounce bottle of water, takes a 500mg tablet, and drives at 35 miles per hour in a car built to metric tolerances. Both sets of units are already there. They are built into everyday vocabulary, used without a second thought.

If you do international business

Date format. This one fails silently, which is what makes it dangerous. The US writes month first, most of the world writes day first, and much of Asia writes year first. So 03/04/2026 is 3 April to a supplier in Manchester and 4 March to a buyer in Chicago. Neither system throws an error. You just get the wrong date, and you find out when the container does not move. The fix is easy and free: apply ISO 8601 yyyy-mm-dd format.

Time zone. Converting to UTC is already work for anyone outside it. The US adds layers on top: multiple domestic zones, then state-level and even county-level exceptions to daylight saving.

Worse, the changeover dates are not aligned internationally. The US and the EU switch on different weekends, so the offset between New York and London is five hours for most of the year and four for a few weeks each spring and autumn. A recurring call booked in local time drifts twice a year, and somebody misses it both times.

Then the 12-hour clock lands on top of that, with the same failure mode as mm/dd. Write 2026-04-03 14:00 UTC and the argument ends.

Currency exchange. The pump is not only a units problem. It is a units problem with a currency problem sitting on it, and currency moves, a lot, in an increasingly volatile market.

What it actually costs

Two things, and they compound.

Errors. Unit mistakes do not degrade gracefully. They produce answers that are confidently and completely wrong. NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter in 1999 because one team worked in pound-force seconds and another in newton-seconds. Air Canada's Flight 143 ran out of fuel at 41,000 feet in 1983 after a pounds-and-kilograms mix-up during that country's metric transition. Both were competent teams doing correct arithmetic.

Frictions. The quieter cost is that every specification gets converted twice, every listing gets dual-labeled, every drawing carries two sets of numbers, and every one of those steps is a place for a rounding error to enter. In sourcing, the version is smaller and more frequent: a tolerance quoted in thousandths of an inch and read as millimeters, a carton dimension that fails the carrier's dimensional-weight rule, a fabric weight ordered in ounces per square yard and produced in grams per square meter. Nobody bills for it. It shows up as slower cycles and reopened POs.

What's the fix

Mentally. Be aware that the US system is the minority position, and hold that awareness lightly rather than defensively. Travel more. Assume the other side is reading their own document correctly. Be explicit.

Practically. Send documents in the units and formats your counterparty already uses, and carry the conversion burden yourself. It is cheaper than a reworked order. If in doubt, refer to ISO standards.

Back to the pump

So: how many people can look at that photo and say what the gas costs in dollars per gallon, in US terms?

The answer is about $0.03/gallon. For comparison, on 2026-08-03, the AAA national average in the US was $4.10, and California, the most expensive state, averaged $5.66.

Again, this article is not about politics. It is about cultural awareness. But you can extrapolate and conclude as you want.
