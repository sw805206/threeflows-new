Setting Up a Company: Plan Before You Act

Filing the paperwork takes an afternoon. The decisions that determine whether you filed the right thing take longer — and skipping that planning step is the single most common mistake founders make.

February 17, 2026

Why Formalize at All

Three reasons founders actually give, in order of how often they matter in practice:

The identity shift. A registered entity is what turns "I sell things on the side" into a business, in your own head as much as anyone else's. That shift shows up in decisions — how much you reinvest, how seriously you negotiate with suppliers — well before it shows up on paper.

Deduction legitimacy. An unformed individual can still deduct legitimate business expenses, but a formal entity with its own bank account and books makes the line between personal and business spending unambiguous — to you, and to anyone who later asks.

Credibility that compounds. A business bank account, a loan application, a vendor account — all of these move faster with a registered entity behind them, and none of that credibility is retroactive. The earlier you form, the earlier the clock starts.

When: Is This You

If you've been running a side project as an individual and are now ready to formalize it, this is for you. The mechanical part — filing with a state, getting an EIN — is fast and cheap. The part worth slowing down for is upstream of that: which structure, which state, and what follows the filing. Get those wrong and the fix later costs far more than the hour you'd have spent thinking it through now.

One timing note: if you're reading this in January, you likely avoided forming in December — most states charge close to a full year's fee regardless of when in the year you file, so a few weeks' delay saved you a near-duplicate payment. If you did file in December, nothing is wrong; your CPA will just confirm whether you're on a calendar year (Jan–Dec, the default) or a fiscal year (any other 12-month period), since that determines when your first return is due.

What: Entity Type

The common types, and where each one fits:

  • LLC (Limited Liability Company) — the default for most small businesses. Flexible ownership, liability protection, pass-through taxation by default. Fits almost any single-owner or small multi-owner business that isn't raising outside capital.
  • LLP (Limited Liability Partnership) — for professional service partnerships (law, accounting, architecture, medicine, in states that permit it) where each partner needs protection from the others' malpractice, but the group still wants pass-through taxation. Many states restrict LLPs to licensed professions — check availability before assuming it's an option.
  • C-Corp — the default for a business that plans to raise institutional or venture capital. Double taxation (see below), but the share structure, stock classes, and governance investors expect.
  • S-Corp — not actually a separate entity type: it's a tax election layered on top of an LLC or a C-Corp. It lets eligible owners split income into salary and distribution, reducing self-employment tax once profit clears a reasonable-salary threshold. Covered in more depth under Who below.
  • Non-profit — for a mission-driven organization with no owners or shareholders. Pursuing federal tax-exempt status (501(c)(3) or similar) is a separate process from state formation, and disqualifies the profit-distribution model entirely — this path is only relevant if distributing profit to owners was never the goal.

Decision factors, in the order they usually matter:

  1. Who's involved. Number of owners, their citizenship, and whether outside investors are part of the plan all narrow the field before tax treatment even enters the conversation — see Who below.
  2. Single vs. double taxation. LLCs, LLPs, and S-Corp elections are taxed once, at the owner level. A C-Corp is taxed twice — once on corporate profit, again when that profit is distributed to you.
  3. Governance formality. An LLC has minimal ongoing paperwork — most states just want an annual report. A corporation (C or S) requires more formal governance regardless of size: board and shareholder meetings, minutes, and documented resolutions for major decisions. This is a real, ongoing cost — someone has to actually do it, or the liability shield it's meant to support gets weaker.

Rule of thumb: if you don't know yet, default to an LLC — it's simpler to run and easier to convert later if your situation changes. The exceptions: a licensed-professional partnership should look at an LLP if the state offers it; a business planning to raise outside capital should expect to end up a C-Corp; and a mission-driven organization with no profit-distribution intent belongs in the non-profit path from day one.

Who: Citizenship, Ownership Structure, and Investors

Citizenship determines what's on the table. A US citizen or resident can elect S-Corp status once profits justify it — splitting income into salary (payroll tax) and distribution (no self-employment tax), which can meaningfully cut your tax burden once profit exceeds a reasonable salary. It's an election you make once the numbers work in its favor, not a formation-day decision, and your CPA should run that math. A non-US citizen doesn't have the S-Corp option; the realistic choices are an LLC or a C-Corp, depending on whether you're planning to raise outside capital.

Single-member vs. multi-member changes the tax default, not the liability protection. Both give you the same liability shield. A single-member LLC is taxed like a sole proprietorship — profit flows straight to your personal return, no separate business return required. A multi-member LLC defaults to partnership taxation, with profit and loss split among members per the operating agreement and its own informational return.

If outside investors are part of the plan, that usually forces the decision. Institutional investors typically require a C-Corp — the share structure, stock classes, and governance venture investment assumes. If you're not raising outside money, this consideration doesn't apply yet, and the LLC default above stands.

Where: State of Formation

Delaware, Nevada, and Wyoming get recommended constantly — for reasons that mostly don't apply to a new small business. Delaware's advantage is a deep body of corporate case law and a specialized business court, which matters to venture-backed companies expecting future litigation or investor due diligence. Nevada and Wyoming's pitch is no state income tax and strong privacy protections. Both are real advantages — for the businesses they're built for.

If you're not sure yet and just getting started, form in your home state. Registering out of state when you don't operate there usually means you'll still have to register as a "foreign entity" in the state where you actually do business — so you end up paying two states' fees instead of one, for a benefit that mostly doesn't apply yet to a company with no investors and no multi-state footprint.

State of formation also determines which state-specific rules apply to you — and these vary more than founders expect:

  • Beneficial-ownership disclosure. New York's LLC Transparency Act took effect January 1, 2026, requiring most LLCs formed or registered there to disclose beneficial owners. Other states are watching New York's rollout before deciding whether to follow. This is separate from — and currently more binding than — the federal requirement below.
  • Federal BOI reporting, by contrast, is currently paused for domestic US-formed entities under a FinCEN rule from March 2025; only foreign-formed entities registering in a US state still file.
  • Sales tax nexus. Where you form isn't necessarily where you owe sales tax — that's driven by where you have "economic nexus" (generally a sales-volume threshold in a given state), which can include states you've never set foot in. Formation state and sales-tax obligations are two separate questions; don't assume forming in one state limits your sales-tax exposure to it.

How: The Tactical Sequence

  1. Lock in the decisions above first — entity type, citizenship path, ownership structure, and state. These are the ones that are expensive to reverse; everything below is easier to fix later.
  2. Set up your privacy layer before you file, not after. A registered agent or virtual mailbox for your business address, a dedicated business phone number, and a business email are all faster and cheaper to arrange before your name and address hit the public record than to retrofit afterward.
  3. Think about your brand at the same time — company name and domain. Your legal entity name doesn't have to match your brand name, but checking domain availability and basic trademark conflicts before you file saves you from filing under a name you'll rename in six months.
  4. Use your CPA if you have one, or a formation service if you don't. If you have a CPA, loop them in at formation — you'll be back in front of them at year-end regardless, and one advisor with the full picture from day one beats a filing decision they have to work around later. If you don't have one yet and want to move now, a formation service (LegalZoom or similar) can file the entity quickly; plan to find a CPA soon after, since formation and ongoing tax strategy are different jobs.
