Cashflow: What Unit Economics Doesn't Tell You

A follow-up to the unit economics post — because a healthy per-unit P&L can still put you deep in the hole before you see your first dollar back.

December 28, 2025

In the last post, we broke down the most fundamental part of the P&L — unit economics — and worked through what it takes to make money on a $2 coaster set or a $20 coffee maker. If you haven't read it, this post picks up where that one left off.

Let's take the $20 coffee maker forward. Assume you sell it for $200 (10× ex-factory) with free shipping. Working through the six variable cost categories, the unit P&L stacks up to a $48 profit at 24% gross margin. On paper, that's a solid business.

But that number is incomplete. The moment you account for when those costs hit versus when revenue arrives, the picture changes.

The Unit P&L Ignores Time

The unit P&L stacks every cost and the revenue into a single column, as if they all happen simultaneously. In reality, those events are spread across months — and the gap between when cash goes out and when it comes back in is where businesses get into trouble.

Assume it takes seven months from placing your first purchase order to your first sale hitting the customer. Suppliers want to be paid when their service is completed. Ads and storage run every month while the goods are in transit and sitting on a shelf. Your marketplace holds your revenue, and returns can settle a month after the sale — so a single unit's cashflow cycle actually stretches across eight months from PO to close.

Read left to right along the waterfall. You place the purchase order in Month 1. By Month 6 — before a single dollar of revenue arrives — you are $142 in the hole on this one unit. The sale happens in Month 6; the revenue hits your bank account in Month 7, finally turning you net positive. In Month 8, the 5% return probability catches up, and you settle at the $48 profit that the unit P&L predicted from the start.

That's an eight-month cashflow cycle on a single unit. The unit P&L says $48 profit. The cashflow timeline says you're down $142 before you make that $48. That gives you an ROI of $48 ÷ $142 = 33.8%. Extremely healthy — a genuinely lucrative business, if you have the working capital to run it.

Now Multiply by 100 Units

Here's where it gets more complicated.

If you're buying and selling 100 units, is it true that you'll need $142 × 100 = $14,200 in working capital, and at the end you'll pocket $48 × 100 = $4,800?

No. The cash gap will be much larger than $14,200.

You have to buy and ship all 100 units at once — the ex-factory, ocean, and tariff costs land as one lump in Month 1 or 2. But you only sell them one at a time. If you move 10 units a month, it takes ten months to work through the batch — and by Month 8 you'll need to place the next purchase order, so a new $142 hole opens on 100 more units while the first batch is still selling through.

Do the math for yourself, or come to one of our seminars where we work through it together. Either way, the takeaway is the same: batch buying and per-unit selling mean the cash trough scales worse than linearly with volume.

And Then There's Everything Else

None of the above accounts for the costs of running the business, as opposed to selling the unit.

On an annual basis, for example, you have entity filing fees (amount varies by state and entity type) and CPA service fees. On a monthly basis, you're paying the Amazon Professional Seller plan (a flat $39.99, separate from the 15% referral fee that lives in the unit P&L), a business phone line, a mailbox, office or storage rent. Per-shipment or per-batch you're paying for thermal labels, UPS barcodes, carton boxes, bank transaction fees. And there are compliance costs — the ones you can't afford to skip, because the fine is worse than the fee. Every business will have its own list.

Some of these are truly fixed — you pay them whether you sell one unit or a million. Others are variable but on their own schedule. Some you can absorb by doing yourself; others you want a professional to handle because the cost of getting them wrong is much larger than the cost of doing them right.

Don't let the startup urge push you into spending on the wrong things. Cash is king; every penny counts. But don't skimp on the things where DIY costs you more than it saves — CPAs, entity setup, and compliance are near the top of that list.

What to Take Away

Unit economics tell you whether a product can make money. Cashflow tells you whether you can afford to run the business that produces it. Both matter — one without the other is a partial picture.
