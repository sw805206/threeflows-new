Sell Fast or Sell High: A Livestream Selling Model

A client wanted to know two things before his first livestream sale: how low his price can go, and how fast he has to sell. It turned out neither question has an answer on its own.

May 17, 2026

Whatnot is a livestream selling app, best known for live auctions. A seller goes live, talks through the product, and takes bids in real time while buyers comment and check out inside the stream. It has grown fast — merchants were expected to move over $6 billion on the platform in 2025, and roughly one in five US digital shoppers now buys something off a livestream.

Two months ago, a client came to us excited about it. His product had gone stale through his existing channels and this looked like the way out. He had done the back-of-the-envelope version already: buy cost is low, and an aggressive $15 — 70% off his $50 list — still leaves room, so as long as the units sell, he makes money.

But it also felt uneasy. What he could not do was hold four moving parts in his head at once — price, speed, platform spend, and the person on camera — and see which one to pull.

So we built him a model before he started, then helped him use it to steer while he was live. The process was fascinating.

What He Wanted to Know

The client would sign up with the platform and hire two influencers. He knew he needed to sell fast, and that a lower price helps. But he needed quantified measures —

How fast does he need to sell, and how low can the price go.

What We Knew

Input Value
Landed cost, all in $3,500
Units bought / sellable 500 / 490
Cost per sellable unit $7.14
Platform fees and traffic $70 per hour
Influencer $20 per hour, plus 15% of revenue
Outbound shipping A wash — excluded

What Nobody Knew

Three things, and no amount of modeling beforehand could produce them.

How $70 an hour translates into people watching. How price changes the rate people buy at. Whether one influencer converts better than another.

Nobody knew, because nobody had done it before. These could only be measured, and only by going live. So the model makes them variables, and the client uses the data he collects to extrapolate forward.

That is not a reason to skip the model. It is the reason to build one. A model does not guarantee you anything. It tells you how the variables relate, and how a change in the inputs moves the result you care about most.

The model guides the execution. The execution refines the model. Repeat.

Before: The Plan

This is the output of a simulation model. It shows him the relationship between the speed to sell and the breakeven price — as these are the two levers his influencers can manage.

No surprise — the faster one can sell, the fixed cost (platform fee and labor cost, $90/hr total) can be spread thinner, making the breakeven price lower. The hope is that a lower price boosts sales, but one can only lower it to a certain threshold.

Since nobody knows the actual speed, or the prices the audience is willing to pay, this graph is there to enable the team to act quickly during the livestream.

During: The Refinement

Session 1 2
Influencer A B
Hours 8 6
Total sold 124 119
Selling price $19.95 $18.00
Speed 15.5 19.8
Profit $497.02 $430.70
Breakeven price $15.23 $13.74
Breakeven speed 9.2 11.0

Influencer A ran eight hours and sold 124 units, an average of 15.5 an hour at $19.95 each. Based on the model, breakeven speed at $19.95 is 9.2 an hour — meaning if he wants to maintain this price, he needs to sell 9.2 units per hour.

An influencer can influence the selling speed but cannot control it. Price was the only lever the client held directly. So the boss set the new price at $18 and handed the next shift to influencer B, knowing that at $18 the speed threshold rises to 11.0.

While A and B were live, the client watched the speeds. With the model already built, he knew that at A’s speed the breakeven price is $15.23 and at B’s it is $13.74 — and as they slowed or sped up, he entered the new speed and had the new breakeven price immediately. He could tune up and down in real time.

After: The Validation

On the surface, B won everything.

Per hour A B Winner
Units 15.5 19.8 B, +28%
Revenue $309.23 $357.00 B, +15%
Profit $62.13 $71.78 B, +15%

Three metrics, three wins, no argument. And all three share the same blind spot: they measure how hard an hour was worked, not what each unit earned on its way out the door. B sold cheaper. Per-hour metrics reward her for the discount and never charge her for it.

Now divide by units instead of by hours, and the ranking inverts.

Per unit A B Winner
Profit $4.01 $3.62 A, +11%

Same two sessions. Same numbers. A wins by 11%.

Neither metric is wrong. They answer different questions, and which one is honest depends entirely on what is scarce.

Profit per hour is right when hours are scarce and stock is replaceable — you can reorder inventory, you cannot buy back a Saturday night. Profit per unit is right when stock is scarce and hours are not: every unit is irreplaceable, so you want the most out of each one even if it takes longer.

The client has 490 units to clear. He can buy platform hours whenever he likes. The lot is not being reordered. Stock is the constraint. Which makes profit per unit the honest metric here, and makes A the better session.

If that argument feels abstract, here is the concrete version. Give A and B the same job: clear the entire lot, all 490 units, each at the price and speed they actually achieved. Now it is like for like — same product, same quantity, the same lot of stock consumed. The only things that differ are the price each charged and the hours each took. Whoever ends with more money won.

  • A’s way, $19.95 at 15.5 an hour: 32 billed hours, about $1,929
  • B’s way, $18.00 at 19.8 an hour: 25 billed hours, about $1,747

A takes seven hours longer and finishes about $182 ahead. Same shelf, same cost of goods, more money at the end of it. That is the whole comparison, and A wins it.

None of which makes the $18 decision a bad one. Cutting price to move faster is a sound instinct, and it was made mid-stream on a single night’s data with the clock running. The point is only that there was $182 sitting on the other side of it, and the model could have surfaced that in the moment — if anyone had said out loud that the lot, not the calendar, was the thing being managed.

From B’s side of it: at $18 there were two bars she could have been held to. She needed 18.7 an hour to match A’s profit per hour, and 21.7 an hour to match his profit per unit. She did 19.8. She cleared one bar and missed the other — and when stock is the limit, the one she missed is the one that mattered.

And none of this makes B the loser

She was asked to beat a speed target, at a price she was given, and was then measured against a different metric entirely, on one night of data. That is not analysis. It is a verdict wearing analysis as a costume.

She may also have drawn a harder room, or an easier one — nobody knows, because nobody measured the room. She went second, having watched A work. The shelf behind her held 366 units, not 490, which is a different selling problem.

Answering it properly would mean designing for it — fix the price, alternate A and B across comparable slots, repeat until the noise washes out. That costs selling hours. Whether the answer is worth the hours is a business judgment, not a modeling one.

We think this is the most useful thing the model did all week, and it did it by refusing to conclude. Instead it exposed a process flaw upstream of itself. The model was flexible enough — it answers per hour or per unit equally well, and it would have answered either one on request. What was missing was the objective. Nobody had said which constraint the business was actually managing, and until that is named, a model will hand you a perfectly correct number that points at the wrong decision.

The Human Side

Everything above is the lab-rat way of putting it, and it is not the whole story.

Before A and B ever went live, there was nothing to compare. You cannot rank two people you have not watched work. Then you watch one session and you do form a view — the energy level, the savviness in converting a hesitant buyer, the instinct for when to push the price. That view is real information, and it arrives faster than any dataset.

But it is a hypothesis, not a conclusion. You will like the person who reminds you of yourself. You will remember the one great moment and forget the flat hour. The evidence does not always support the feeling, and when it does not, the feeling is usually the thing that is wrong.

So the two work together. The data is there to confirm what you observe. The data is also there when you cannot observe.

Being fair to your numbers and being fair to your people turn out to be the same discipline. A model that tells you what you cannot yet claim is doing exactly the job you built it for.

The team spent the first session’s $497 on a late-night dinner and drinks right after the first 8 hours. All of it.

That was the right call. It was not about profit, it was tuition, and it was team building. What they were celebrating was not the money — it was that after eight hours they finally knew the number nobody could have told them in advance, and they had learned how to hold a room they could not stand in.

The Point

None of this is really about livestream selling.

A model does not tell you what will happen. It tells you which number decides the outcome, so you know what to watch and what to do when you see it. Before the stream it turned breakeven from a price into a curve. During the stream it turned one session’s results into the next session’s price. And at the moment it would have been easiest to draw a conclusion about a person, it showed exactly why the evidence would not carry one.

Build it, act on it, measure, feed it back. Do by what you learn, and learn from what you do. Not just the product. Not just the process. The people as well.
